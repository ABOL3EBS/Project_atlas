# Photovoltaic Systems

## Module efficiency

Monocrystalline silicon modules are the most efficient commercially available
technology, with typical peak efficiency between 19 and 22 percent. Polycrystalline
modules trail at roughly 15 to 17 percent. Thin-film cadmium telluride modules are
the cheapest per watt to manufacture but usually sit at 12 to 14 percent efficiency,
which means they need more roof area for the same output.

## How solar cells convert light to electricity

Each cell contains a semiconductor junction of N-type and P-type silicon. Photons
strike the junction and knock electrons loose, creating a direct current. The current
flows through a junction box, then into an inverter that converts the DC output to
grid-compatible alternating current at 50 or 60 hertz. The inverter also implements
maximum power point tracking so the array always operates at the voltage and current
combination that produces peak output.

## Temperature derating

Solar panels are rated at standard test conditions of 25 degrees Celsius and 1000
watts per square meter of irradiance. Panels lose roughly 0.3 to 0.5 percent of rated
output for every degree the cell temperature exceeds 25 degrees. In hot rooftop
installations cell temperatures can climb to 65 degrees, which can cost an array more
than ten percent of its nameplate output on the hottest afternoons.

## Degradation and lifetime

Manufacturers typically guarantee that a panel retains at least 90 percent of its
initial power after ten years and 80 percent after 25 years. Most failures are caused
by microcracks, hotspots, and moisture ingress through the back sheet, rather than by
the solar cells themselves. Cleaning the array with deionized water and a soft brush
a few times a year prevents soiling losses that can otherwise reach several percent.

## Grid connections

Small residential arrays connect behind the electric meter under net metering, where
surplus production is credited on the monthly bill. Larger commercial arrays connect
directly to the distribution network and require a certified inverter, a disconnect
switch, and a production meter. Interconnection approval and export limits are set by
the local utility and usually take a few weeks to process.

## Sizing a system

A home typically needs an array producing roughly the annual electricity it consumes,
offset slightly for the difference between peak solar hours and evening usage. The
sunniest months produce far more than winter months, so systems are sized against the
winter minimum rather than the annual average on battery-backup installs. A useful
rule is to budget three to four kilowatts of panels per thousand square feet of
rooftop, tempered by orientation, tilt, and shading from trees and chimneys. Shading
on even one string can cut output disproportionately because a shaded cell drags down
every cell in its series string.

## Inverters and system architecture

String inverters serve a whole array from one unit and are the cheapest option, but
they stop producing if any panel in the string is shaded. Microinverters attach to each
panel and keep the rest of the array alive when one module is impaired, at a higher
cost per watt. Power optimizers sit between the two: each panel carries a DC optimizer
while a single central inverter converts to AC. Every upgrade in granularity adds
monitoring granularity as well, since per-panel telemetry is what reveals a failing
diode or a delaminating cell quickly.
