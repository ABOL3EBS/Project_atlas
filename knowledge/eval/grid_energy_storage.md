# Grid Energy Storage

## Pumped hydro

Pumped hydroelectric storage moves water between two reservoirs at different
elevations. During surplus generation, pumps push water uphill into the upper
reservoir; when the grid needs power, the water flows back down through turbines.
It is by far the largest-capacity storage technology deployed worldwide because the
basins are cheap to build at utility scale once a suitable valley exists. Round-trip
efficiency is typically 70 to 80 percent, meaning a fifth to a third of the absorbed
energy is lost in pumping and generation. Response time to full discharge is minutes,
which makes it a mismatch for frequency regulation but a good fit for daily load
shifting.

## Flywheels

Flywheel storage keeps energy as rotational kinetic energy of a spinning mass
suspended on magnetic bearings in a vacuum chamber. Charge and discharge are very
fast and the cycle life is effectively unlimited, so flywheels excel at frequency
regulation and smoothing the brief flicker from passing clouds. The trade-off is low
total capacity and a high standstill loss as friction and windage drain the wheel.
A single flywheel installation might hold a few megawatt-hours while a pumped hydro
plant stores thousands, which limits flywheels to short-duration ancillary services
rather than bulk shifting.

## Compressed air storage

Compressed air energy storage compresses ambient air into an underground cavern or a
pressurized vessel and releases it through a turbine when generating. Conventional
natural gas heaters boost the expanding air to improve efficiency, while adiabatic
designs capture the heat of compression and reuse it, reaching higher round-trip
efficiency without fuel. Compression raises pressure and temperature together, which
is what makes the stored heat a reusable resource rather than waste. Siting requires
either a salt cavern or the capacity to hold pressure safely, which constrains where
the technology can be built.

## Role in a renewable grid

Storage buys time while the renewable fleet rebalances. Solar rides the midday peak
and collapses at sunset, so a storage plant charged at noon can flatten the evening
ramp. The economic case depends on how many hours of discharge the grid needs, the
price spread between cheap and expensive hours, and the round-trip efficiency of the
technology chosen. Batteries pair with frequency response because they respond in
milliseconds; bulk storage such as pumped hydro pairs with daily energy shifting.
Most grids end up with a portfolio of durations because no single technology serves
both a two-second hiccup and a two-week calm period.

## Cycle life of non-battery technologies

Non-electrochemical storage systems age in ways that differ from batteries. A
pumped hydro basin degrades only when silt fills the reservoir or corrosion weakens the
turbines, and the cycle life is in the tens of thousands of fill-and-drain cycles over
decades of service. Flywheel rotors fatigue through millions of spin cycles, and the
vacuum chamber maintenance is what keeps the standstill loss acceptable. Compressed air
systems rely on geological sealing, so the number of charge cycles may depend more on
mechanical fatigue of the compressors and valves than on the capacity of the
underground storage volume. Lifecycle cost is therefore dominated by maintenance
frequency rather than the one-time construction cost.
